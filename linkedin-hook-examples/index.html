LinkedIn hook examples, and how to write one

The first line of a post decides whether anyone reads the second. Here are 63 hook examples in nine types, why each type works, a short method for writing your own, and a free way to see how yours looks above “…see more”.

What a LinkedIn hook is

A hook is the first line or two of a post. In the feed, LinkedIn shows only the start of a longer post and puts a “…see more” link after it. Whatever sits before that link is what a person reads while deciding whether to open the rest.

That makes the hook a small job with one purpose: make the right reader curious enough to tap. It doesn’t need to be clever. It needs to be clear, specific and true to the post underneath.

How to use the examples

Every hook below is a pattern to copy the shape of, not the words. Swap in your own story, number or result.

Then paste your version into the free hook preview and check how it looks above “…see more”.

63 LinkedIn hook examples, by type

Nine types, seven examples each. Each type starts with why it works, so you can pick the one that fits what you have to say.

  • Story hooks

    Why it works: A story starts in the middle of something, so the reader wants to know how it ends. Name a moment, not a theme.

    • I almost deleted the proposal an hour before the deadline.
    • My first client fired me by text message, on a Sunday.
    • The room went quiet when I said our price out loud.
    • I sat in the parking lot for ten minutes before the interview.
    • Three years ago I answered a stranger’s question in a comment. It changed my job.
    • The email had one line: “Can we talk today?”
    • I forgot my slides, so I talked for twenty minutes with a marker.

  • Contrarian hooks

    Why it works: A claim that goes against the usual advice makes people stop and check whether they agree. It only works if you can back it up in the post.

    • Stop writing a new post every day.
    • Your personal brand is not your logo.
    • Most networking advice only works if you’re already well known.
    • You don’t need a niche to get started. You need a first customer.
    • A bigger audience won’t fix a weak offer.
    • “Always be closing” is bad advice for anyone selling a service.
    • Posting less made my posts easier to remember.

  • Number hooks

    Why it works: A specific number sets expectations: the reader knows how much time this will take and that something concrete is coming. Use a number you can actually deliver.

    • Six questions I ask before I take on any project.
    • We spent $400 on tools last year and kept two.
    • It took 11 drafts to get this one-page résumé right.
    • Five minutes at the end of Friday saved me a day each month.
    • I ran 23 interviews this quarter. Three answers came up every time.
    • One spreadsheet column fixed our late invoices.
    • Four slides are enough to explain most ideas.

  • Question hooks

    Why it works: A question pulls the reader in by asking for an answer in their head. Ask something they can answer from their own week, not a riddle.

    • When did you last say no to a client?
    • What would you stop doing if nobody noticed?
    • Why do most meetings end with no decision?
    • What does your first week at a new job really look like?
    • Who taught you the thing you use most at work?
    • What is the one email you keep putting off?
    • If you could only keep one tool, which would it be?

  • Before and after hooks

    Why it works: Two states side by side show change without a long explanation. The gap between them is the story.

    • One year ago: 3 followers who weren’t family. Today: a full inbox of replies.
    • Before: a 40-slide deck nobody read. After: five slides and a decision.
    • My old résumé listed duties. My new one lists results.
    • Our onboarding used to take three weeks. Now it takes four days.
    • My first post got two likes. This is what I changed.
    • Last spring I dreaded Monday. This spring I plan it on Friday.
    • Then: every quote built from scratch. Now: one template and ten minutes.

  • Mistake hooks

    Why it works: People read about mistakes to avoid their own. Owning the error also makes you more believable than listing wins.

    • I priced my first workshop too low, and it cost me more than money.
    • My biggest mistake as a new manager: I fixed things for people.
    • I sent the wrong file to a client. Here’s how I handled it.
    • I wrote “open to work” on everything and got nothing back.
    • We launched without asking one customer first.
    • I said yes to every request for a year. It was a mistake.
    • My worst hire taught me how to write a job post.

  • Lesson hooks

    Why it works: A lesson hook promises a takeaway and names where it came from. Starting with the source makes a plain idea feel earned.

    • Eight years of client work, and one habit I’d keep.
    • What a failed product launch taught me about listening.
    • The best advice my first manager gave me fit in one sentence.
    • Ten interviews on the other side of the table changed how I answer questions.
    • Burnout taught me what “urgent” really means.
    • A customer’s complaint taught me more than any course.
    • I learned this from watching a nurse run a shift.

  • How-to hooks

    Why it works: A how-to hook says what the reader will be able to do afterward. Name the result and the effort, and leave out the buildup.

    • How to write a project update in under five minutes.
    • How to ask for a raise without a long speech.
    • How I turn one long client call into four posts.
    • How to follow up after a job interview, in three lines.
    • How to say no to a meeting and keep the relationship.
    • How to explain your job to someone outside your field.
    • The simple way I plan a month of posts in one hour.

  • List hooks

    Why it works: A list hook promises something easy to scan and easy to save. Make the items real and few enough to read in a minute.

    • Five things I put in every client proposal.
    • Seven small habits that made me a better manager.
    • The tools I use every week, and the ones I dropped.
    • Three questions that make a networking call useful.
    • Eight posts I’m glad I wrote, and one I wish I hadn’t.
    • Six lines from my cover letter that got replies.
    • The ten words I cut from every draft.

All examples are invented for this page. Replace the details with your own, and never claim a result you didn’t get.

How to write a LinkedIn hook

  1. Write the post first. The hook should promise what the post delivers. It’s easier to find the real promise once the post exists.
  2. Pick the most interesting sentence. Look through your draft for the line you’d tell a friend over coffee. That line, or a tighter version of it, is usually the hook.
  3. Be specific. “A big client” becomes “my biggest client.” “Some tips” becomes “four tips.” Details make a line believable.
  4. Use plain words. Read it out loud. If you wouldn’t say it to a colleague, rewrite it.
  5. Put the point before the cut. Don’t save the interesting part for line four. Check where “…see more” falls on a phone before you post.
  6. Write three versions. Try a story, a number and a question for the same post, and keep the one that sounds most like you.

Before and after

Before: I want to share some thoughts on something I’ve been learning about feedback lately.

After: The worst feedback I ever gave was also the kindest.

The second version shows a person, a tension and a reason to read on, in one line.

Common hook mistakes

  • Warming up

    “I’ve been thinking a lot about…” uses the best space in the post to say nothing. Start with the thought itself.

  • Promising and not delivering

    A hook that overpromises gets the tap and loses the reader. If the post is a modest tip, write a modest hook.

  • Hiding the point behind the cut

    If the one interesting thing comes after “…see more”, most people never reach it. Move it up.

  • Starting with your name or title

    Your name is already above the post. Use the line for the idea.

  • Too many ideas at once

    A hook that tries to cover three points reads as a muddle. One idea, one line.

  • Copying a formula word for word

    Readers have seen the same opening many times. Keep the pattern and use your own detail.

  • Cliffhangers with no payoff

    “You won’t believe what happened next” teases without telling. Say what the post is about, then tell it well.

  • Never checking the preview

    A hook that reads well on your laptop can be cut in a different place on a phone. Check it before you post.

Questions

Hook questions

What is a hook on LinkedIn?

The first line or two of a post, the part people read before “…see more”. Its job is to make the right person curious enough to open the whole post.

How long should a LinkedIn hook be?

Short enough to read in a few seconds and to show before the cut on a phone. One or two short lines is usual. Test yours in the free hook preview, because the exact cut depends on the device and your line breaks.

What makes a good LinkedIn hook?

It is specific, uses plain words, and promises what the post delivers. A real moment, a concrete number or a clear claim usually works better than a vague teaser.

Should I use a question as a hook?

Sometimes. A question works when readers can answer it from their own week. A question they can’t answer, or one you answer in the next line, adds nothing. Try a story or a number if the question feels forced.

Is it okay to copy a hook example?

Copy the pattern, not the words. Put your own story, number or result in it. A hook that claims something that didn’t happen costs you trust.

Can I use the same hook structure every time?

You can, but readers notice repetition. Rotate between a few types, such as a story, a number and a mistake, and see which ones feel most natural to you.

How do I check where “…see more” will cut my post?

Use the free hook preview for an approximate view on a phone. LinkedIn decides the exact cut, so treat it as a close guide, not a guarantee.

What should come right after the hook?

The next line should deliver on it: the story’s first beat, the first item of the list, or the reason behind the claim. Skip the warm-up and keep going.
