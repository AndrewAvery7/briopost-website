LinkedIn poll ideas, and how to write a good poll

A good poll asks one clear question that people can answer in a second and still want to talk about. Below are poll ideas sorted by purpose, how to write the options, and what to do when the poll closes.

What makes a good poll question

A poll is the shortest kind of post to answer. That only works if the question is easy to read and easy to pick from.

  • One question, one idea. “Which tool and why?” is two questions. Ask one, and save the “why” for the comments.
  • Answerable from experience. Ask about what people do or prefer, not what they would need to look up.
  • Options that don’t overlap. Every voter should find exactly one answer that fits.
  • A real reason to ask. Ask something you want to know, and say what you’ll do with the answers.
  • Short. LinkedIn limits the length of the question and of each option, so plain words win.

A quick test before you post

Read the question out loud to someone. If they can answer before you finish reading the options, it’s ready.

If they ask “what do you mean?”, rewrite the question, not the options.

20 LinkedIn poll ideas, by purpose

Start from the reason you’re asking. Swap in your own field’s words, and keep each option short.

  • Learn about your audience

    • Where do you get most of your industry news?
    • How many people are on your team?
    • Which part of your week takes the longest?
    • How do you prefer to learn a new skill?
    • Which tool could your team not work without?

  • Start a friendly debate

    • Is a four-day week realistic in our field?
    • Cover letters: still worth writing?
    • Should job posts show the salary range?
    • For a first hire: skills or attitude?
    • Office, hybrid or remote for a new starter’s first month?

  • Test an idea

    • Which topic should my next guide cover?
    • Which of these two titles would you click?
    • Would a monthly checklist be useful to you?
    • How do you like tips: a carousel, a short post or a video?
    • Which question should I answer in next week’s post?

  • Follow up a post

    • Which of Monday’s three tips will you try first?
    • Last week I shared a checklist. Have you used it yet?
    • You said the hardest part is pricing. Which fix should I explain first?
    • Did my post about meetings match your experience?
    • Should I write a part two?

Stay away from questions about health, religion, politics and other personal matters. LinkedIn’s rules don’t allow polls that ask for sensitive information (see below).

Writing the options

  • Make them distinct. Ranges like “1–5” and “5–10” overlap at 5. Write “2 to 9” and “10 to 49” instead.
  • Put them in a natural order. Smallest to largest, earliest to latest, or the order people would think of them.
  • Keep them the same shape. If one option is a noun, make them all nouns.
  • Leave a way out. When answers really vary, make the last option “It depends” or “Something else” and ask for the details in the comments.
  • Skip the joke option unless it’s an answer someone would honestly pick. A joke option makes the results harder to read.

The post text above the poll

A LinkedIn poll is part of a post, and the post text sits above the question. Use it to give the poll a reason:

  • Say why you’re asking, in a sentence or two.
  • Add the context people need to answer, such as who the question is for.
  • Ask for the story behind the vote: “Tell me why in the comments.”
  • Say when you’ll share what you learned, and then do it.

Keep it short and easy to scan. Our guide to LinkedIn post formatting covers line breaks, lists and the “see more” cut.

What to do after the poll closes

  1. Read the comments as well as the numbers. The reasons people give are often more useful than the percentages.
  2. Write a follow-up post. Share the result, what surprised you and what you’ll do next.
  3. Be honest about what it is. A poll is a quick read of the people who voted, not a survey. Say so when you share the numbers.
  4. Turn the result into something to keep. One clear number makes a good single LinkedIn graphic, and the reasons from the comments can become a carousel.

Plan the follow-up when you plan the poll. If it goes on your content calendar for the day after the poll closes, you won’t forget it.

LinkedIn’s own poll rules

According to LinkedIn Help’s polls FAQ (checked November 17, 2026):

  • The question can be up to 140 characters, and each option up to 30.
  • You can add up to four options on desktop and Android, and up to five in the iOS app.
  • A poll can’t be edited once it’s created, though you can delete it.
  • A closed poll can’t be reopened.
  • The author can see how each person voted. Voters see the percentages after they vote, and everyone sees them once the poll closes.
  • Polls may not ask for sensitive information, such as about health, religion or politics.

LinkedIn can change these rules, so check the Help page before you rely on a number.

Questions

Poll questions

How many options can a LinkedIn poll have?

Up to four on desktop and Android, and up to five in the iOS app, according to LinkedIn Help’s polls FAQ (checked November 17, 2026). Briopost’s poll editor takes 2 to 4.

Can I edit a LinkedIn poll after posting it?

No. LinkedIn Help says a poll can’t be edited once it’s created, though you can delete it. Read the question and options once more before you post.

Can people see how I voted?

The poll’s author can see how each person voted, according to LinkedIn Help. Other voters see only the percentages.

How long should a LinkedIn poll stay open?

Choose the length that fits when you plan to share the results. LinkedIn offers one day, three days, one week or two weeks. If your follow-up post is planned for next week, a one-week poll closes in time.

What should I do with the results?

Share them in a follow-up post: the numbers, what surprised you and what you’ll do next. Say that a poll is a quick read of the people who voted, not a survey.

How do I check the text above my poll?

Paste its first lines into the free hook preview on this page. It shows roughly where “…see more” cuts the text on a phone, so you can keep the version that sets up the question first.
